We keep one notebook of everything we learn about growing an online store. It is
a folder of plain pages, each on one thing, linked by name. This post is the map
of that notebook: the kinds of pages in it and how they connect, so that a person
preparing a first call can walk from "who is this company" to "what would we sell
them, and why would it work" by following links.

## Why a notebook

We tried a database first. It held facts well, but nobody preparing a call wanted
to read one. A notebook reads the way a strategist thinks: open the company, see
the problem it faces, click through to the fixes for that problem, and see which
tests showed a fix working somewhere else. Every sentence on a page says where it
came from — a job posting, the company's own site, a published test — and a
sentence that is our own judgment says so ("our call").

The notebook is also the shared memory for our agents. They read from it, and
they write the drafts a person then reviews.

## The kinds of pages

There are about a dozen kinds of page, each in its own folder, and each with the
same outline every time, so a reader always knows where to look.

| Kind | One page per | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Companies | business we look at | Who they are, how they grow, who runs growth, how they measure it |
| Company audits | company | What it is trying to grow, what is broken on the way, and what would fix it |
| Prospects | company we pursue | Why them, where they stand with us, what we have said, what would move them on |
| Concepts | problem or method | Who it affects, what it means for us, how to measure it |
| Metrics | number a business is judged on | What it measures and how |
| Techniques | way to raise conversion | Where it lives, what it moves, how to test it, what goes wrong |
| Experiments | change being tested | The hypothesis, who ran it and what each found, where it worked and where it did not |
| Case studies | one company, one situation | What they did and what it shows |
| Learnings | lesson that held across companies | What backs it, what cuts against it, what it means for us |
| Blogs | source we read | Whose it is and how far to trust it |
| Tools | piece of software | What it is used for, who uses it, what it costs |
| Business strategy | question about us | Who we are, whom we sell to, how we price |
| Agents | agent, built or planned | What it does, where it runs, whether it is built |

## How the kinds link

The kinds are not a filing system; they are a chain. A company faces a problem
(a concept). A technique fixes that problem. An experiment tests the technique,
and the companies that ran it are rows on the experiment's page. When several
experiments and case studies agree, the lesson gets its own page, and lessons
shape both the techniques we recommend and the strategy we sell by. The audit is
where that chain meets one company: it names the problem and recommends the
techniques.

Two small rules make the chain work. First, **an experiment is one page per
change, not one per company**: "show delivery dates on the product page" is one
page, and every company that tested it is a row with what it found. That is what
lets a pitch say "this worked in these settings and not in those" instead of
quoting one lucky test. Second, **a lesson is titled as the lesson** — the page
name is the claim — so the list of learnings reads as what we believe.

## Walking it before a call

A strategist preparing a first call starts on the prospect page, opens the
company, and reads the audit. The audit names the company's biggest problem and
links the concept page, which links the techniques that fix it, which link the
experiments that tested them. Four clicks go from "who are they" to "here is a
fix, and here is where it has worked before". Nothing in that walk is a query;
it is reading.

## Keeping it honest as it grows

A notebook that grows goes stale in quiet ways: two pages that disagree, a fact a
newer source has overtaken, a page nothing links to. So every page is generated
from a record that carries its sources, and a set of checks runs over the whole
notebook: every link lands, every quoted sentence is really in the source it
cites, every diagram draws. A second pass has fresh agents read the notebook the
way a strategist would, ask it questions, and report what they could not answer
from the pages alone. What they report becomes the next work.

The page that describes the notebook's shape — the one this post is drawn from —
is itself generated from the same tables the renderer uses, so the map cannot
drift from the territory.
