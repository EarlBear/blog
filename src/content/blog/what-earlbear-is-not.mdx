*New here? [Start with the engineering arc](/blog/start-here-engineering) — what EarlBear is and the words the other posts assume. This post is the other half of that: what we are not.*

Five names came up in the founders' thread as companies a customer might file us
next to: **Apollo.io, Instantly, lemlist, Clay, and Sales Navigator.** We researched
all five — their sites, their sitemaps, their pricing meters, their founders, and
what the market says about them when they aren't in the room. The findings are in
the appendix.

The short version is that the confusion is real but shallow. All five are stops on
one assembly line, and the line ends in the same place.

## The line all five are on

Outbound has a settled shape. Find a buyer. Resolve their contact details. Send
them a sequence. Hope for a reply. Each of these five owns a different stretch of
it, and the market's own comparison articles say so plainly — the usual advice is
not "pick one" but "Clay enriches, Instantly sends, use both."[^stack]

That last node is the whole argument. **Every one of these five companies has a
product that ends when a stranger replies.** Their use-case pages, their pricing
meters, and their case studies all measure the same thing: messages out, replies
in, meetings booked.

EarlBear has no product before that moment and all of its product after it.

## Where our loop actually runs

EarlBear runs storefronts. [The fleet](/blog/the-agent-fleet) audits a live store
as a first-time customer with intent to buy, records what got in the way of a sale,
and — for stores we run — ships reversible A/B experiments against the live site,
keeping the winners and rolling back the losers.

the interesting work is downstream of it.

Two rows are worth reading twice.

**"Sends on your behalf" inverts.** Every one of them will send for you; we
specifically will not. Envoy drafts and queues, and a person sends — that is the
compliance boundary, stated first-class in its brief. The single capability this
market is built around is the one we designed ourselves out of.

## What a pricing meter admits

**"What it meters" is the sharpest fact in the whole study.** A pricing meter is a
company saying out loud what it thinks it sells. Apollo meters exported records per
salesperson. Instantly meters emails sent. Clay meters enriched rows. Navigator
meters a person's access to a graph. None of them can meter an outcome, because
none of them owns one. We meter a store.

## The one overlap worth naming

Say it plainly rather than pretending there is none: **Clay is a genuine near-miss,
and it is worth understanding why.**

Clay ships agents — Claygent — that browse the live web, navigate pages, fill forms,
and extract structured findings across thousands of domains in parallel. Described
at that altitude, that is Scout. Both of us run fleets of browsing agents against a
list of domains and write structured rows.

The difference is what the row is *for*:

| | Claygent | Scout |
| --- | --- | --- |
| whose site it visits | the prospect's, to describe them | the store's, to fix it |
| what it looks for | a datapoint a rep can quote | friction that costs a sale |
| what it produces | a cell in a table | a finding another agent acts on |
| who consumes it | a salesperson writing a first line | Medic, shipping an experiment |
| measured by | reply rate | conversion rate, weeks later |

Clay's agent produces a fact about a stranger. Ours produces a change to someone's
revenue. Same verb, different object.

There is also a quieter agreement worth borrowing: Clay's public argument for why
their agent works is that its **domain is deliberately narrow** — research companies
and people for go-to-market, nothing else — because a constrained domain is what
makes evaluation possible.[^clay-narrow] That is the same argument behind our
one-job-per-agent fleet, arrived at independently. When the company at a $5B mark
has published our architecture rationale, that is corroboration, not competition.[^clay-tender]

## The second-order overlap: they might be suppliers

Envoy's contact-discovery step is genuinely open — the design says so. Discovering
the business contact behind a storefront is exactly what Apollo and Clay sell, and
both expose an MCP server. The honest framing for a call is:

> Those are tools our outreach agent might one day *buy from*. They are not
> alternatives to what we do.

## How to answer it on a call

Three sentences, in this order:

1. **Name the line.** "Apollo, Clay, Instantly — those all help you start a
   conversation with a stranger. They're very good at it."
2. **Name the boundary.** "Every one of them stops when the stranger replies. That's
   where our work starts."
3. **Name the object.** "We don't sell you a tool to run. We run your storefront —
   audit it, test changes on live traffic, keep what wins, roll back what doesn't.
   You're buying a conversion rate, not a seat."

If the prospect pushes — "but you said you have AI agents that scrape sites" — the
answer is the Claygent/Scout table above: same verb, different object. Their agents
look at *your buyers*. Ours look at *your store*.

## What this means for positioning

Three things fall out of the research and belong in the plan:

- **Stop using the word "leads" first.** It is the single word that puts us on this
  assembly line in a listener's head. Lead generation is one scaffolded step of one
  of our four agents. Lead with the storefront.
- **"We never send" is a differentiator, not a caveat.** In a market where the
  top-rated sender is publicly fighting deliverability complaints and has itself
  been called out for spamming from eight domains, a human on the send side reads as
  a design choice, not a missing feature.
- **The comparison we want is not Clay.** It is
  [the growth team you would otherwise hire](/blog/life-without-earlbear) — that post
  prices the alternative we actually replace. None of these five replace a CRO
  consultant, a frontend engineer, and an analyst.

<Fold id="apollo" name="Apollo.io" note="the database with a sequencer bolted on">

> "The AI sales platform for smarter, faster revenue growth"

Founded 2015 in San Francisco as **ZenProspect** by Tim Zheng, Ray Li (ex-Square),
and Roy Chung; YC W16; rebranded to Apollo.io in June 2018.[^apollo-origin] Roughly
1,600 employees, up from about 1,000 in 2023.[^apollo-latka] Raised $250M total,
last a $100M Series D at a $1.6B valuation led by Bain Capital Ventures with
Sequoia, Tribe, and Nexus participating.[^apollo-series-d]

The origin is worth knowing: Zheng balked at a $9,000/year ZoomInfo quote while
selling an education product and built prospecting tooling for himself.[^apollo-origin]
That explains the entire pricing posture.

**What they sell.** A database — "240M+ contacts and 30M+ companies," "over 600,000
companies using Apollo" — wrapped in the tooling to act on it: search, sequences, a
dialer, meeting booking, call recording, CRM enrichment, plus an API and an MCP
server.[^apollo-home] Their own framing is *Build → Execute → Improve*.

**Use cases, verbatim:** Outbound ("turn hours of prospecting into minutes"),
Inbound ("qualify and act on inbound leads in seconds"), Data Enrichment, Deal
Execution.[^apollo-solutions] The sitemap has **no `use-cases/` section at all** — it
organizes by *persona* instead, with six pages for sales leaders, AEs, sales
development, RevOps, marketers, and founders. It is sold to a job title.

**Pricing.** $49/$79/$119 per user per month annually (Basic/Professional/
Organization; $59/$99/$149 monthly), with 30,000 / 48,000 / 72,000 credits a year and
a 3-seat minimum on Organization — a $4,284/year real floor.[^apollo-pricing] A
credit burns when you *export a contact out of Apollo*: 1 for an email, 5 for a
mobile, $0.20 per overage. The meter is **records exported, per seat**.

**What search found.** A split rating: 4.7/5 on G2 across ~9,645 reviews against
**2.9/5 on Trustpilot** across ~1,049, the latter dominated by billing disputes,
account suspensions, and data-accuracy complaints.[^apollo-reviews] Practitioners
report bounce rates well above Apollo's claimed ~91% email accuracy, with r/coldemail
citing 32–38% bounce on "verified" exports through early 2026, and materially weaker
coverage outside North America.[^apollo-accuracy] Support is their lowest-rated G2
sub-category.

</Fold>

<Fold id="instantly" name="Instantly" note="a sending machine">

> "Reach Clients On Autopilot"

Founded 2021 by Nils Schneider and Raul Kaevand. **Bootstrapped — no outside
capital.** Roughly $38M estimated ARR in 2026, up from $20M ARR in December 2024;
headcount self-reported at 51–200.[^instantly-latka]

**What they sell.** Deliverability, really: unlimited mailboxes, an automated warmup
network, inbox rotation, inbox-placement testing, a lead database, an email verifier,
a unified inbox, a light CRM, and a 2026 agent layer (AI Sales Agent, AI Reply Agent,
Lead Finder Agent). Claims: "50,000+ sales teams," "45,000+ outbound teams," "50B+
emails."[^instantly-home]

**The sitemap is the finding.** 1,930 URLs, of which **1,395 are blog** and exactly
**one** is a product page. No use-case section, no solutions section — the go-to-market
is programmatic SEO, hundreds of `/prompt/personalized-cold-email-opener-for-<vertical>`
pages. That is a content machine with a product attached.

**Pricing.** Two ladders. Outreach plans run $47 / $97 / $358 a month for 5,000 /
125,000 / 500,000 emails; bundle plans that add the agents run $94 / $194 / $555 a
month.[^instantly-pricing] The meter is **emails sent** — not seats. They price
outbound volume.

**What search found.** Deliverability is both the product and the top complaint.
Multiple 2026 r/coldemail threads report warmup heat scores reading 90+ while live
campaigns hit 30–40% spam placement, and done-for-you domains arriving with poor
reputation is the most-cited Q1 2026 complaint pattern.[^instantly-review] A
first-page search result for their own brand is titled *"Instantly.ai Just Admitted
Their Warmup Network Got My Brand New Domains Reputation Blocked."* They have also
been publicly called out for spamming a recipient from eight different
domains.[^instantly-spam] Notably, they list **Clay and Apollo in their own
integration marketplace** — confirming the stack model rather than competing with it.

</Fold>

<Fold id="lemlist" name="lemlist" note="multichannel, and the best-mannered of the five">

> "Cover your TAM with AI agents that research, personalize, and engage prospects on autopilot."

Founded 2018 in Paris by Guillaume Moubeche, part of the **lempire** portfolio
(lemlist, lemwarm, Taplio, Tweet Hunter, lemcal). Around 160 employees and roughly
$53M ARR in 2026, up from about $40M in 2025. Bootstrapped; the $30M raised in 2021
was a **secondary** at a $150M valuation — founders selling shares, not new capital
into the company.[^lemlist-latka]

**What they sell.** A 650M+ lead database, email finder and verifier, multichannel
sequences across email, LinkedIn, SMS, WhatsApp and calls, a unified inbox,
deliverability setup (lemwarm), intent-signal agents, and lemAgent — a conversational
campaign builder. They also ship a CLI, an MCP server, and a `/claude-skills/`
library, which is worth reading as prior art regardless of the competitive
question.[^lemlist-home]

**lemAgent's approval gate is the closest thing in this cohort to our own rule**, and
deserves to be quoted rather than paraphrased:

> "Nothing launches without your approval. lemAgent hands you the full campaign for
> review: the brief, the list, the sequence, and every message. Edit what you want,
> approve the rest, and launch without leaving the chat."[^lemagent]

The instinct is the same as Envoy's. What differs is what sits on either side of the
gate: lemlist gates *a campaign to strangers*; we gate *a message about a specific
storefront problem we found by walking that storefront*. And after their gate, the
tool sends. After ours, a person does.

**Pricing.** $55/user/month annually for Email (50,000 emails), $87/user/month for
Multichannel, with data sold separately as credits — 1 credit = $0.01, a verified
email is 5 credits, a phone number 20, an intent signal 20 to 400.[^lemlist-pricing]
The meter is **seat × channel**.

</Fold>

<Fold id="clay" name="Clay" note="the near-miss">

> "Infrastructure to get any data, run agentic workflows, and launch GTM plays."

Founded June 2017 in New York by Kareem Amin and Nicolae Rusan, who met at McGill and
started at Microsoft together; they built Frame (acquired by Sailthru in 2012), after
which Amin was VP Product at the Wall Street Journal and Rusan VP Product at Dow
Jones. Varun Anand joined as head of operations in late 2021.[^clay-contrary] About
1,000 employees in 2026, up from ~299 in 2024.[^clay-tracxn] A $100M Series C led by
CapitalG (Alphabet) at a $3.1B valuation, and a January 2026 employee tender offer at
**$5B** led by DST Global, on roughly $100M ARR.[^clay-series-c][^clay-tender]

**What they sell.** A table where each row is a company or person and each column is
an enrichment — a data provider from a 200+ vendor marketplace, an API call, a
formula, or an AI agent. Four layers: data infrastructure, agents (Claygent, an agent
CLI/API, an MCP for reps), orchestration, and execution (ad sync, a native
sequencer). "Trusted by more than 500,000 leading GTM teams," with the strongest logo
wall of the cohort: Stripe, OpenAI, Anthropic, Figma, Notion, HubSpot, Google,
Workday, Uber, Siemens.[^clay-home]

**Clay is the only one of the five with a real use-case map** — 13 pages: Outbound,
CRM enrichment, Data enrichment, Inbound enrichment, ABM, TAM sourcing, Account
research, Rep prospecting, Rep assist, Territory planning, PLG assist, Reverse
ETL.[^clay-usecases] Read them together and the pattern is total: every one is about
*learning something about a buyer*. Not one is about changing anything on the
customer's own property. Clay's output is always a field in a row.

**Pricing.** Free, then from $167/month (Launch) and $446/month (Growth), on **two
separate meters** — data credits buy the data itself (from $0.05 each) and actions buy
the platform work (under $0.01 each).[^clay-pricing] Seats are effectively free. The
meter is **enriched rows**.

**What search found.** Clay is treated as the assumed center of the modern stack
rather than a competitor to anyone — "Clay and Instantly are designed to be used
together" is the standard advice, Instantly lists Clay in its marketplace, and the
canonical Clay-vs-lemlist article concludes they are complementary.[^stack] The
recurring practitioner complaint is credit burn: web-scraping columns consume budget
fast.

</Fold>

<Fold id="navigator" name="LinkedIn Sales Navigator" note="not a product in the same sense">

Microsoft-owned, launched 2014. There is no founder story and no separately
disclosed revenue, and that is itself the finding: Navigator is a subscription line
inside a platform monopoly, priced against an asset nobody can reproduce — 1.3B+
members and 64M+ company pages.[^navigator-graph]

**What they sell.** Access, not automation: 40+ search filters, unlimited searches
capped at 2,500 results per query, saved lead and account lists, real-time buyer
intent alerts, Smart Links, CRM sync on higher tiers, and 50 InMail credits a
month.[^navigator-features]

**What it does not do** is the entry's whole point. No bulk email — outreach is
InMail, inside LinkedIn, capped at 50 a month. **No CSV export**, which is precisely
why a scraper economy (Evaboot, PhantomBuster, Wiza, Kanbox, Skrapp) exists around
it. No automated connection requests or messages. A hard 2,500-profile ceiling per
search. Nothing outside the member graph.[^navigator-limits]

**Pricing.** Core at $119.99 per seat monthly ($89.99 annually), Advanced at $159.99
($149.99 annually), Advanced Plus custom at roughly $1,300–1,600 per seat per
year.[^navigator-pricing] The meter is a **seat**, flat, regardless of use — the
oldest model in the cohort, and it works because the underlying asset is not
reproducible.

**What search found.** The most common thing written about Sales Navigator is how to
get data *out* of it. That tells you its real role: the seed list somebody else
enriches and somebody else sends.

</Fold>
